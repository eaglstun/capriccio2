# Canon and lineage

Where the aesthetic came from, so choices can be grounded in something rather
than in a Pinterest board.

## Origin, roughly 2010–2012

- **Chuck Person's Eccojams Vol. 1** (2010) — Daniel Lopatin, better known as
  Oneohtrix Point Never. Chopped, pitched-down, looping snippets of 80s pop.
  The audio grammar of the genre: take a commercial artifact, slow it, loop the
  part that never resolves.
- **James Ferraro — Far Side Virtual** (2011). Ringtones, café ambience,
  Skype chimes; consumer-tech sound treated as composition.
- **Macintosh Plus — Floral Shoppe** (2011), by Ramona Xavier / Vektroid. The
  album that fixed the visual template: flat pink field, a Greco-Roman marble
  bust, a small black-and-white cityscape, katakana. Almost every vaporwave
  image since is in conversation with that cover.
- **Adam Harper, "Vaporwave and the pop-art of the virtual plaza"**, _Dummy_,
  2012 — the first serious critical writeup, and the source of the framing of
  vaporwave as ambiguous critique rather than straight nostalgia.

Adjacent and immediately prior: **chillwave** (~2009, hazy sun-faded
nostalgia) and **seapunk** (~2011, Tumblr, cyan, dolphins, 3D-render kitsch).
Seapunk's visual DNA is thoroughly mixed into vaporwave's.

## Visual sources it steals from

**Memphis Group** — Ettore Sottsass and collaborators, Milan, 1981. Squiggles,
confetti terrazzo, primary shapes, deliberately impractical furniture. The
source of the squiggle-and-triangle pattern language that reads instantly as
"80s" and appears on every laundromat table and Solo cup of the period.

**Hiroshi Nagai** — Japanese illustrator whose flat, cloudless pool-and-palm
paintings covered a lot of 1980s city pop. If a vaporwave image has an
impossibly clean swimming pool under an impossibly blue sky, it is downstream
of Nagai.

**Patrick Nagel** — the stylized flat-planed women of 80s American commercial
illustration, best known from Duran Duran's _Rio_ cover.

**Japanese city pop** generally — Mariya Takeuchi, Anri, Toshiki Kadomatsu.
Its late-2010s YouTube revival ran alongside vaporwave and cross-pollinated
with it; the katakana in vaporwave art is largely borrowed from those sleeves.

**The mall.** Atrium fountains, terrazzo floors, brass railings, potted ficus,
food-court signage, and Muzak — the actual Muzak Corporation, whose piped
"stimulus progression" programming is what mallsoft is imitating. Dead-mall
photography (Seph Lawless and others) supplied the melancholy version.

**The desktop.** Windows 95/98 chrome, Mac OS 7 through 9, Kai's Power Tools,
WordArt, 3D Studio Max chrome-and-lens-flare title cards, Poser figures,
After Dark screensavers, GeoCities under-construction GIFs, CompuServe and AOL
onboarding screens. Software-box art from when software came in boxes.

**Early 3D games.** PlayStation 1 and Sega Saturn: low-poly, wobbling
vertices, swimming textures, hard fog to hide the draw distance. The
limitations became the style.

**Consumer objects as icons.** Fiji water, Arizona iced tea, cassettes, CRTs,
Pepsi logos of the correct vintage, product photography lit like devotional
painting.

**Classical statuary** — plaster-cast Helios, Venus de Milo, David. The joke
is the mall's habit of using Western antiquity as free prestige decor, and the
sincerity is that it does look good.

## Later mutations

- **Simpsonwave** (2016) — slowed Simpsons clips over vaporwave tracks, with
  VHS grade and lens wobble. Where the "damaged tape" treatment got codified.
- **Mallsoft** — vaporwave with all people removed: long reverb, distant
  muzak, empty atrium. The most emotionally serious branch.
- **Faux-corporate / "utopian virtual"** — invented companies, fake product
  lines, fake terms of service, plausible logos for nothing.
- **Broken transmission / hypnagogic** — signal loss, test cards, emergency
  broadcast, the aesthetic drifting toward horror.

## Text and language

The spaced-out `ａ　ｅ　ｓ　ｔ　ｈ　ｅ　ｔ　ｉ　ｃ` treatment comes from
Unicode fullwidth forms (U+FF01–U+FF5E), which exist so Latin letters can
occupy one CJK character cell. Using them was originally a way to make
Latin text look like it came off a Japanese system — which is exactly the
displacement the genre is about.

Track and album titles favor: untranslated Japanese, corporate boilerplate,
trademark symbols, dates in the near past, and words like _plaza_, _virtual_,
_business_, _lifestyle_, _infinite_, _420_.

## The thing to actually understand

Vaporwave is about a promise that was made and not kept — that consumer
technology and global commerce were going to deliver a frictionless, gleaming
future. It takes the leftover marketing material for that future and plays it
back at the wrong speed. The affection is real, the disappointment is real,
and neither one wins. Work that only mocks its material comes out thin; work
that only celebrates it comes out as a stock-photo gradient.
