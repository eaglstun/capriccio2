CAPRICCIO 2

a city of arches, at the end of humanity

What this is

A city builder. You raise the architecture — piers, spans, stairs, vaults — and you do not place a single house. The citizens decide what to do with the spaces you leave them. Roof a terrace and someone moves in. Bring water somewhere and the neighbourhood turns toward it.

The architecture emits pockets: habitable voids scored on shelter, light, outlook and distance to water. Everything that happens afterwards follows from those numbers. You are never asked to build a home, only to make somewhere worth living.

Where it came from

CAPRICCIO was made by Ethan Mollick with Fable, one of a series of city builders generated in the manner of particular artists. That one took Giovanni Battista Piranesi — the eighteenth-century engraver whose imaginary Roman ruins were always grander and more impossible than anything that stood. A capriccio is exactly that: a painting of buildings that never shared a horizon.

It shipped as a single 662KB file. No images, no audio, no fonts, no models — every stone, every citizen, every sound generated in code at load. It would fit twice over on a floppy disk.

This version

The same game, at the end of humanity.

Not a sequel and not a remake. Every rule underneath is untouched — the scoring, the growth, the save format, the way citizens choose where to live. What changed is the world those rules run in.

The ruins you inherit are concrete and rebar rather than marble. Overpasses stand on the ridge with the roadway gone. A pipe still drains into the canyon, staining the rock, with nobody upstream. A tower crane holds a pallet of blocks it was carrying when the work stopped. Satellites cross overhead on fixed tracks, some tumbling, still transmitting. And the billboards are still lit, still cheerful, still selling to an empty plain.

The point is how little is different. The citizens still want shelter, light, water, and somewhere to gather in the evening. Ten thousand years, and the list is the same list.

Things you might not notice

  • The birds became drones. Same schedule, same two-to-four calls, same falling pitch — a square wave where there used to be a sine.
  • Marcus is still Marcus. His trade changed. Tullia still talks about the gardens up there.
  • SOULS never changed. Everything around it went technical and that one word stayed.
  • A quarter can still be named for candles, one entry away from a quarter named for sodium lamps. Place names outlive the things they were named for.
  • The oldest fabric still carries antique stone, reused in newer walls the way late Rome reused what it found.
  • The five meters are the original five. ACCESS, SHELTER, LIGHT, BELONGING, GRANDEUR — needs do not date.
  • Between the title and the city, two lines appear. They are real comments from the sky shader — notes the program wrote to itself about how to draw its own sunset, which survived only because comments inside strings are not stripped.
  • Only sixteen plates survive a save. Engrave a seventeenth and watch which one leaves the record. Choosing what remains is the closest this game has to a goal.
  • The five requests run out. After that the only way to earn the clearance to build further is to publish what you have already made.

Colophon

Renders
three.js r180. Engraved hatching projected in world space, so the strokes sit on the stone instead of crawling across the screen.
Dither
The world is dithered against a blue-noise threshold generated at load — aperiodic, clustered, biased toward edges, an homage rather than a copy. The plates are the real thing: true Atkinson error diffusion, six neighbours at one eighth each and two eighths thrown away, exactly as it ran on a Macintosh in 1984.
Sound
Entirely synthesized. Pink-noise wind gusting on a fourteen-second cycle, water that fades in with the square of the distance, and a bell whose upper partials decay first because that is what struck metal does.
Score
Written in Strudel — the JavaScript port of Alex McLean's TidalCycles, a language for describing music as patterns rather than sequencing it note by note. The piece is four chords in A minor that take half a minute to decide they want percussion, suspend their thirds for a while, and borrow exactly one major chord, late. No samples: every voice is generated.
Walkthrough
Six beats, played inside the game. It advances when you actually do the thing, never on a timer, and the camera yields the moment you touch it. What you build during it is real and stays.
Assets
None. There is nothing to download but the code.
